You’re not less productive because you lack willpower. Often you’re working against the biology of your own brain, pushing it to do more. Find out how to work with it instead.

Content partner

You go back to the same email several times. By midday, it’s harder and harder to focus. That’s just biology. Your brain doesn’t work at the same intensity around the clock. The better you know its rhythm, the easier it is to work, learn, rest and make decisions.

In this multimedia project, you’ll see what happens in the brain from waking up to falling asleep, and learn how to use and support your body’s natural rhythm. It’s a practical guide to 24 hours in the life of your brain, based on the latest science and expert commentary.

The brain doesn’t work the same way around the clock. Its different areas peak at different times of the day and night.

See what you can expect from your brain at the time shown on the clock at the top of the page.

For this graphic, we chose the brain areas worth highlighting at each time of day. That doesn’t mean other areas aren’t active at the same time. The times apply to people with an intermediate chronotype. In “larks”, these processes happen earlier; in “owls”, later.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus is the body’s main biological clock, located in the hypothalamus. Its neurons are most active during the day. Morning light reaching the retina resets this clock every day, suppresses melatonin and prepares the body for activity.

The prefrontal cortex handles planning, working memory and decision-making. How well these functions work depends on the time of day and on how many hours have passed since waking up. In people with an intermediate chronotype, they usually peak before noon; in “owls”, a little later.

The reward system, which includes the nucleus accumbens, is a set of brain structures responsible for motivation, learning goal-directed behaviour and feeling pleasure. It, too, has a daily rhythm.

Self-reported positive feelings (good mood, energy) usually peak in the afternoon. But one MRI study of 16 young men is worth mentioning. Their brains responded to reward most weakly around 2 p.m., and more strongly in the morning and evening. The researchers explain that the brain responds more strongly to pleasures it doesn’t expect.

Activity in the prefrontal cortex dips. This can show up as a temporary drop in alertness and the ability to focus. The dip comes from the daily rhythm. It can happen without lunch, even when someone doesn’t know what time it is, but a carbohydrate-rich meal makes it worse.

The motor cortex plans, controls and carries out voluntary movements of the skeletal muscles. For many people, late afternoon brings the shortest reaction times and the best coordination.

It’s worth stressing that when you’re in top form depends mainly on how long it’s been since you woke up, not on the time on the clock.

The default mode network (DMN), which includes the medial prefrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex, is an integrated network of brain areas that is most active when we’re not engaged in a specific task aimed at the outside world. That’s when our minds start to wander and we tend toward deeper reflection.

Studies show that at a time that isn’t optimal for us (evening for a “lark”, early morning for an “owl”), unexpected associations come more easily. But the idea that the DMN is responsible for our creativity is still a hypothesis.

The pineal gland plays a key role in syncing the body with the cycle of light and dark. At a signal from the biological clock, it starts releasing melatonin, the hormone of “biological night”. In dim light, this happens on average about two hours before your usual bedtime.

Even ordinary room lighting in the evening can delay and shorten melatonin release. Hours of bright screens shortly before sleep have a similar effect.

In deep NREM sleep, mostly in the first half of the night, the hippocampus “replays” memory traces from the past day. Synchronised brain waves strengthen them and gradually store them in the neocortex. This is called memory consolidation.

The glymphatic system is a network of spaces around blood vessels through which cerebrospinal fluid flows through brain tissue. Research suggests that this flow increases in deep sleep, which may help clear out waste products. The mechanism is still debated by scientists.

During REM sleep, activity in the amygdala and the visual cortex is very high, hence vivid dreams. Activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, by contrast, drops markedly, which may explain the weak logical control and critical judgment of dream events. REM sleep dominates the second half of the night.

At dawn, light reaches the retina again, the suprachiasmatic nucleus suppresses melatonin, and a new day begins.
