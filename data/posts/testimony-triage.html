As a part of the investigative team, I built an internal AI-powered research tool to analyze a volume of documents and detect possible crimes against humanity in testimonies of illegally displaced children.

Challenges with the initial data

The transcripts came in with no speaker labels.

People switch between Ukrainian and Russian, use slang, and the timestamps come every two or three sentences.

No audio, so I couldn’t diarize to find out who was speaking.

The idea

For the investigation we needed to find the episodes where something relevant to crimes against children was said and determine which violation each one might be.

The challenges with analysis

The judgment depends mostly on the witness's answer, and a short answer like "Yes" only means something next to the question that came before it. So before I can classify anything, I have to work out who is speaking and which sentences are related to each other.

The transcripts contain names and birthdates of minors, so I can't send whole documents to a model. At the same time we have to be able to check every suggestion against the original text.

A raw transcript looks like this:

[00:12] Did they make you go to school there? Yes. What did you study? Russian history. Did you have Conversations About Important Things? Yes.

Low-level design

Anonymisation: first, real names are swapped for tags like PERSON_1

Speakers labeling: a batch of timestamped sections plus the last two turns already labelled (to provide context) are sent to the model. The returned turns are aligned back to the original with word alignment.

Classification: the labelled turns are cut into chunks of about 200 words, ending before an interviewer turn, and each window is classified. Each call gets a taxonomy injected, where each crime is described and proof-read by a lawyer. The model returns JSON-schema structured findings which makes the prompt shorter and the answer structured. It returns the issue, the exact quote, and what the witness actually says (supported, uncertain, hearsay or denied).

How I tested it

The first test measured the relevance of the passage against a human verified example.

The second one checked the correctness of the category assigned to the passage.

High-level architecture

The flow is: upload, ingest (txt, docx or pdf), redact names, label speakers, cut windows, classify, add rule-based leads, remove duplicates, anchor to the original, map to crimes, save.

I built it as an app to enable the team to upload files and keep each person's review decisions saved. Clicking a suggestion highlights the passage in the original. Each suggestion can be kept, dismissed or given a note, and the list can be exported to CSV.

The app is one container with a private storage bucket mounted as a folder. Access is one shared passcode plus a display name. The OpenAI key lives in Secret Manager, so no user ever handles it, and the container image contains no transcripts. It runs a single instance, because the store is file-based. After a restart the job resumes without paying for the same chunks twice.

What didn't work

The first version was the YAML taxonomy with keyword dictionaries, regex Q/A markers and sentence-level labels. It separated the interviewer from everyone else reasonably well, but the rest was weak, and a labelled witness and guardian only helped if the roles were right.

I also tried similarity-based chunking and labeling but it worked worse.
