China has moved into our homes for good. The question is no longer whether we depend on Chinese products, but how much. We analysed import data for the objects we use every day. See whether you could furnish a home without products from China—and what that choice would mean.

Take a walk through our virtual house and see how likely each object is to have come from China.

Outside the house is garden equipment. If it is imported, there is nearly a 50% chance that it came from China. Factories in two of the country’s wealthiest provinces, Guangdong and Zhejiang, specialise in these goods. Their catalogues cover everything from barbecues and hammocks to garden furniture, composting systems, sprinklers, solar garden lights and children’s toys for lawns and sandpits.

As we enter, we may literally bump into another Chinese product: the front door. Imports of steel doors from China have been rising for several years. In 2023, one in five imported doors of this type came from China. Nearly 105,000 arrived in Poland in 2024—more than 15,000 above the previous year, according to forumbranzowe.com.

Poland also imports interior doors, sliding doors and windows from China—including floor-to-ceiling “French” windows. Those windows may be covered by Chinese indoor roller blinds or curtains.

Reliable information about the quality of these products is hard to find. No Polish consumer tests were available, so buyers’ experiences and accounts from people familiar with the manufacturing process offer some guidance.

Reddit users discussing renovations in the HomeImprovement community confirm that price is the main competitive advantage. One considered importing five sliding doors after US manufacturers quoted $12,000; comparable doors from China would cost about one tenth as much. But was it worth the risk?

Another user said he ran a Foshan office sourcing aluminium windows and doors for developers and interior designers abroad. He rated Chinese products highly, but advised buyers to verify the dimensions of the aluminium profiles first: reducing their thickness is an easy way to cut costs, and can shorten a product’s life.

He also suggested avoiding locally supplied fittings, hinges and handles, which he considered lower quality, and asking manufacturers to use imported parts. Finishing can be another concern because, as he put it, “Chinese standards for insulation and finishing differ significantly from Western standards.”

He added that Chinese aluminium windows and doors are generally very affordable and that an excellent product is possible with the right supplier—but the price will reflect it. The same principle applies to most goods ordered in China: better quality costs more.

The quality can vary dramatically with the price. A buyer can specify the required standard when ordering, and the manufacturer will use different materials or components to make the same item.

Even higher-quality, more expensive products can still be cost-effective, according to the Reddit accounts. For the price of one door in the United States, a buyer might purchase several in China.

Chinese suppliers also claim they can deliver faster than factories in Poland. A domestic order typically takes two or three weeks, and longer in peak season. Manufacturer websites promise delivery from China in 10–15 days after the details and payment are settled—roughly one third less time.

Back inside, the floors and walls may be finished with Chinese tiles. Imported ceramic products can cost up to a quarter less than Polish-made equivalents.

There have also been safety concerns. In 2008, Gazeta Prawna reported that a privately imported batch posed a risk to health and life. Combined radiation from potassium-40, radium-226 and thorium-228 was as much as twice the permitted level.

Anyone who skips ceramic tiles may still walk on wooden parquet imported from China.

If the house has solar power, its equipment was almost certainly made in Zhejiang, Jiangsu or Guangdong. China produces more than 80% of the world’s photovoltaic panels. According to Wysokie Napięcie, China supplies over 95% of the PV panels installed in the European Union. Low prices encourage investment, and the same applies to the associated hardware.

In the utility room, the electricity from those panels needs inverters. Wysokie Napięcie reported that China supplied 80% of the inverters newly installed in Europe in 2024. That may soon change: the European Commission wants to curb imports over concerns about the security of European power grids.

The boiler or water heater in this room may also have been made in China, as may the radiators.

The same is true of furniture: the hall wardrobe, living-room table, chairs, closet fittings, beds, kitchen cabinets and bathroom cabinets all have a substantial chance of coming from China.

A 2024 report by PKO Bank Polski’s Economic Analysis Department found that 41.6% of furniture imported into Poland came from China. The range includes tables, chairs, beds, wardrobes, upholstered and children’s furniture, garden and kitchen furniture, garage shelving and more. Manufacturers will even make reproductions of antique furniture to order.

Want furniture in the style of Louis XIV or Louis XVI? Chinese factories can make it.

Until recently, that furniture might at least have contained Polish wood. Two years earlier, almost one fifth of Poland’s timber exports went to China. In the first three quarters of the following year, that figure halved, making it unlikely that Polish timber would return to Poland as furniture.

The price again depends on quality, but it can be as much as 50% below that of Polish or other European manufacturers.

Tables in the living or dining room may be covered by Chinese tablecloths. Poland also imports napkins, table runners and similar textiles from China.

In the bedroom are Chinese home textiles: towels, sheets, bedding and blankets. These are among the categories with the highest Chinese share of Polish imports.

What about quality? Buyers say custom-made Chinese furniture depends not only on price, but also on luck. Australian consumers in Reddit’s AusRenovation community reported problems obtaining service when products arrived with defects.

One user warned: “If there is a defect, the supplier will first try to refund only the damaged part, then charge you to ship that part back for repair. … Manufacturers are also reluctant to make a single small replacement part they are not paid for, so repairs can take a long time.”

Another user, who worked for a customs-services company importing from China, warned that kitchen furniture sometimes exceeded chemical limits. He cited toxic formaldehyde used in fibreboard adhesives, ammonium bifluoride used to treat wood, and even carcinogenic asbestos.

Now consider household appliances. There are more Chinese products in this category around us than we might expect. Polish companies can sell them under domestic brands: the logo and packaging are designed locally, while a factory in China makes the product and applies the name. Only a careful buyer notices the small “made in China” label underneath.

Globalisation has reinforced the trend. The Slovenian appliance brand Gorenje, which makes nearly a quarter of Europe’s appliances, came under Chinese ownership eight years ago. It is now part of Qingdao-based Hisense Group in Shandong province, a major television manufacturer in its own right.

Another Qingdao company, Haier, entered Europe and built factories there. It began by licensing technology from a German refrigerator manufacturer, later acquired it, and ultimately became a global brand. In 2011 it also bought the Sanyo appliance business—including factories and research centres—from Panasonic.

Look at the phone and tablet on the Chinese desk. Even if we avoid explicitly Chinese brands such as Xiaomi and Huawei by buying a Motorola smartphone, the company and its logo have been Chinese-owned for years. The same is true of the ThinkPad laptop brand acquired from IBM.

If you reach for water, there is a good chance you will pour it into a Chinese glass. Poland imports not only enamelware—once a domestic speciality—but also steel pots, pans, cutting boards, cutlery, plates, mugs and glasses.

Quality can be uncertain. In the first half of 2026 alone, Poland’s Chief Sanitary Inspector twice warned about Chinese-made goods and ordered their immediate withdrawal. One alert covered five types of drinking glass sold by a well-known Danish retail chain because lead and cadmium could migrate from the glass. The second found the same heavy metals on the rims of Chinese glasses sold by a German-owned discount chain.

Tests published in late 2025 tell a similar story. Independent consumer organisation Pro-Test checked 162 products sold on Temu and Shein; 110 failed to meet EU standards. In other words, two out of three were defective or unsafe. Tests commissioned by Belgium’s Testachats found a 70% failure rate on the same platforms. Common problems included overheating phone chargers and toxic metals or chemicals in jewellery and clothing.

Asia specialist Radosław Pyffel says China is not entirely leaving its low-cost manufacturing model behind, but recent five-year plans have prioritised technology, electric vehicles, AI, robotics and self-sufficiency in technology and raw materials. Product quality and China’s position in supply chains are rising, he argues, especially as the country turns to Global South markets with a growing middle class.

Pyffel traces China’s current position partly to the short-term thinking of American and European corporations. Citing the proverb “a clever rabbit has three burrows,” he argues that companies pursuing the margins available from cheap production largely abandoned diversified supply chains. China gained monopolies or near-monopolies in strategic sectors; Western economies may need about 15 years to rebuild factories, technologies, components and raw-material supply while China continues to advance.

He compares the process with post-war Japan: Japanese electronics first had a reputation for poor quality, then the industry moved into a leading position. China, he says, is following the same path from low-quality equipment to high technology, but has proved much harder for the United States to contain and continues to move towards the global technological frontier.

Even a house built with furniture made only from Polish wood and electronics assembled from Western parts may still contain Chinese components: bolts and screws. Poland imports those from China too.

How we calculated it

Import data come from Eurostat’s Comext database. We matched product codes to the objects in the illustration and rounded label values to whole percentages.

Each figure shows China’s share of imports in that category—not its share of the entire Polish market or the exact proportion of those objects found in Polish homes.
