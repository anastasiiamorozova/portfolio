The beach as a mirror of the Polish tourist

How important is clean water? Must the beach be wide? How much does the choice of restaurants matter? Answers from the project’s calculator did more than identify good places to stay: together they form a portrait of the Baltic holidaymaker, revealing the needs and summer dreams behind each choice.

That visitor is not easy to stereotype—not simply a passive package tourist or a loner escaping the world. The picture is more deliberate and cautious. Communication specialist and tourism-trends researcher Dr Dominik Lewiński describes someone who goes to the sea “not in search of adventure, but for a holiday that works.”

The priority: a holiday under control

Picture a Polish visitor at the Baltic. In the morning, they leave their room or hotel and reach the beach in minutes. There is no search and no wandering. The ideal beach is wide, the water looks clean and a lifeguard is nearby. Changing rooms and clean toilets are available, and a beach bar is close enough for a cold drink. None of this feels like luxury; it is the expected standard.

The rest of the day follows the same logic. There is no need to spend hours choosing dinner: it is enough to know that something suitable will be available. In the evening, there may be a promenade, some greenery and perhaps a café with a view. The visitor does not require a big city, but does not want to feel cut off either. The goal is rest on their own terms, with the practical details under control.

Lewiński connects this preference for standards and routine with limited leave, rising costs and often complicated family logistics. Travellers do not want to gamble with a holiday they worked hard to afford. “We have grown more comfortable and accustomed to having things ready and reliable,” he says. “The holiday has to work.”

The key to a successful holiday: choice

When rain reaches the Baltic and the wind turns cold, good infrastructure provides alternatives: food, entertainment and recreation. Marcin, 44, from Warsaw has returned to Świnoujście with his wife and three children for a decade. He values how easily the family can switch plans—cycling to Germany on connected promenade routes, visiting nearby Lake Wicko, finding a quieter beach around Karsibór, or choosing an aquapark, the turquoise lake and archipelago at Lubin, Peenemünde’s former rocket site or the aircraft museum at Heringsdorf.

He does not mind booking more than a kilometre from the sea. His preferred area, the Platan estate, is about a 15-minute walk from the beach and also makes it easy to reach the centre, the port or the ferry between Usedom and Wolin.

One tourist, two faces

The data and first-hand accounts suggest that the Polish Baltic tourist has two faces that only appear contradictory.

“There is a tension between the need for comfort and accessible services, and the desire for seclusion and renewal,” Lewiński says. Those needs can coexist: “The same person may sometimes want to rest in comfort and at other times more contemplatively.”

Two poles of the ideal beach

That is why Kołobrzeg and Pogorzelica—radically different places—can finish second and third in the same ranking.

Kołobrzeg answers the desire to have everything close at hand: accommodation, food, bathing areas and easy logistics. It is the safe choice for visitors who want certainty that nothing will be missing.

Pogorzelica answers a different version of the same need: equally good, but quieter. Forest, clean water and silence create a sense of considered escape. “The absence of crowds, silence and peace are among the advantages mentioned most often,” Lewiński notes.

They are opposite ends of one shared requirement: quality with confidence.

Why the winning beach won

A third element explains Mielno’s victory. The typical Baltic visitor seeks a middle path—not an overcrowded resort, but not complete isolation either. Mielno does not force one virtue to come at the expense of another. It never dominated a single category, yet combined sufficient infrastructure, accommodation close to the beach and prices that are easier to live with than those of a major resort.

Kołobrzeg offers confidence through completeness.
Pogorzelica offers it through peace and quality.
Mielno offers it through a well-judged compromise.

How our style of rest has changed

This is more than a story about three towns. Lewiński points back to the origins of mass tourism as rest for workers. In France, paid leave existed for selected groups in the nineteenth century, but 1936 brought the first universal entitlement: two weeks of paid holiday and a 40-hour working week. “It was a model of holiday as rest and recovery,” he says. Only later did tourism turn towards intense sightseeing and experience collecting—sometimes producing fatigue rather than renewal.

There are signs of a return to that original function, driven by the pace of life and a growing mental load. Holidays become less an exercise in constant exploration and more a tool for recovery. “Increasingly, we want to rest—not come home exhausted from our holiday,” Lewiński says.

A holiday for switching off, not keeping score

Travellers increasingly choose places and forms of rest that help them slow down, even at the cost of famous attractions or spectacular experiences. Something once treated as a failure—boredom—can become valuable. “Boredom used to be a curse; today it is a luxury,” Lewiński observes. Having nowhere to be and nothing chasing you can be the reward itself. Destinations that let visitors do less may therefore gain an advantage. Half joking, but only half, he concludes that resorts should offer “high-quality boredom.” It may become their most desirable product.
